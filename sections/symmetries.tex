\section{Symmetry for Spintronics}

A symmetry operation transforms the spatial coordinates, momenta, spins, and
external fields of a system.  It is a symmetry of a Hamiltonian only when the
transformed Hamiltonian describes the same physical system.  For a unitary
operation $G$ this condition is
\begin{equation}
    G\mathcal{H}G^{-1}=\mathcal{H},
    \label{eq:unitary-symmetry-condition}
\end{equation}
whereas time reversal is antiunitary and also complex-conjugates scalar
coefficients.

\subsection{Spatial Transformations and Rotation Matrices}

This section uses column vectors and active transformations: the vector moves
while the coordinate axes remain fixed.  By the right-hand rule, a positive
rotation about $z$ is counterclockwise when viewed from the positive $z$ axis.
After a rotation through $\theta$, the Cartesian basis vectors become
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        R_z(\theta)\hat{\mathbf{x}}
         & =\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \\
        R_z(\theta)\hat{\mathbf{y}}
         & =\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+\theta) \\
                \sin(\frac{\pi}{2}+\theta) \\0\end{pmatrix}
        =\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\\0\end{pmatrix},    \\
        R_z(\theta)\hat{\mathbf{z}}
         & =\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:z-axis-rotated-basis}
\end{equation}
To see why, note that a unit vector in the $xy$ plane at azimuth $\phi$ has
Cartesian components $(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)^{\mathsf T}$.  Initially,
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ have azimuths $0$ and $\pi/2$,
respectively.  An active rotation through $\theta$ adds $\theta$ to each
azimuth, giving $\theta$ and $\pi/2+\theta$; the identities
$\cos(\pi/2+\theta)=-\sin\theta$ and
$\sin(\pi/2+\theta)=\cos\theta$ then give
Eq.~\eqref{eq:z-axis-rotated-basis}.  The vector $\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ is
unchanged because it lies along the rotation axis~\cite{ee237-week11}.
Figure~\ref{fig:z-axis-rotated-basis-projections} shows the corresponding
Cartesian projections.

The columns of a transformation matrix are the transformed basis vectors.
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    R_z(\theta)=
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\
        \sin\theta & \cos\theta  & 0 \\
        0          & 0           & 1
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    R_z(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
    =\begin{pmatrix}
        x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta \\
        x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta \\
        z
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:z-axis-rotation-matrix}
\end{equation}
These conventions and the spatial transformation rules below follow the EE237
symmetry notes~\cite{ee237-week11}.

A passive rotation instead rotates the coordinate axes while leaving the
physical vector fixed.  It is represented by the inverse active rotation,
\begin{equation}
    R_z^{-1}(\theta)=R_z^{\mathsf T}(\theta)=R_z(-\theta).
    \label{eq:active-passive-z-rotation}
\end{equation}
Thus an unexplained sign difference between two rotation matrices often comes
from using active and passive conventions, respectively~\cite{ee237-week11}.
Figure~\ref{fig:active-passive-z-rotation} summarizes the two viewpoints.

A spatial transformation that preserves lengths and angles is represented by
an orthogonal matrix,
\begin{equation}
    R^{\mathsf T}R=I.
    \label{eq:orthogonal-spatial-transformation}
\end{equation}
Taking determinants of both sides gives
\begin{equation}
    \det(R^{\mathsf T}R)=(\det R)^2=1,
    \qquad \det R=\pm 1.
    \label{eq:orthogonal-determinant}
\end{equation}
The sign distinguishes whether the transformation preserves or reverses the
handedness of the coordinate system.  In three dimensions, a proper rotation
preserves handedness and has $\det R=+1$.  A mirror reverses one direction and
has $\det R=-1$.  Full spatial inversion is represented by $R=-I_3$ and also
has $\det R=(-1)^3=-1$~\cite{ee237-week11}.

For example, setting $\theta=90^\circ$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}\to\hat{\mathbf{y}},
    \qquad
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}\to-\hat{\mathbf{x}},
    \qquad
    \hat{\mathbf{z}}\to\hat{\mathbf{z}},
    \label{eq:z90-basis-transformation}
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    R_z(90^\circ)=
    \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & -1 & 0 \\
        1 & 0  & 0 \\
        0 & 0  & 1
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    R_z(90^\circ)
    \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}-y\\x\\z\end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:z90-rotation-example}
\end{equation}
Expanding the determinant along the first row,
\begin{equation}
    \det R_z(90^\circ)
    =0-(-1)
    \begin{vmatrix}
        1 & 0 \\
        0 & 1
    \end{vmatrix}
    +0
    =+1.
    \label{eq:z90-rotation-determinant}
\end{equation}
Thus the matrix is a proper rotation~\cite{ee237-week11}.

For comparison, reflection across the $xy$ plane and full spatial inversion
are represented by
\begin{equation}
    R_{xy}=\begin{pmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0 \\0&1&0\\0&0&-1
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    R_{\mathrm{inv}}=\begin{pmatrix}
        -1 & 0 & 0 \\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:mirror-inversion-matrices}
\end{equation}
Since the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal
entries,
\begin{equation}
    \det R_{xy}=(1)(1)(-1)=-1,
    \qquad
    \det R_{\mathrm{inv}}=(-1)(-1)(-1)=-1.
    \label{eq:mirror-inversion-determinants}
\end{equation}
The inversion result depends on dimension: $\det(-I_n)=(-1)^n$.  In two
dimensions, inversion therefore has determinant $+1$ and is equivalent to a
$180^\circ$ rotation; in three dimensions it has determinant $-1$
~\cite{ee237-week11}.

\subsection{Polar and Axial Vectors}

Position $\mathbf{r}$, momentum $\mathbf{p}$, electric field $\mathbf{E}$, and
charge-current density $\mathbf{J}$ are polar vectors.  Orbital angular momentum
$\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$, spin $\mathbf{S}$, magnetization
$\mathbf{M}$, and magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ are axial vectors.  For an
orthogonal spatial transformation represented by $R$, the two vector classes
transform as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}'=R\mathbf{v},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{a}'=(\det R)R\mathbf{a},
    \label{eq:polar-axial-transformation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}$ is polar and $\mathbf{a}$ is axial.  Thus they transform in
the same way under proper rotations ($\det R=+1$) and differently under mirrors
and inversion ($\det R=-1$)~\cite{ee237-week11}.

The most frequently used transformations are summarized below.  The entries
describe the vector attached to the spatial point after that point has been
mapped by the operation.

\subsection{Time-Reversal Symmetry}

Time reversal changes $t\to-t$ and reverses momenta and angular momenta.  Spin
is an intrinsic angular momentum, so
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}\mathbf{S}\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-\mathbf{S},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{T}i\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-i.
    \label{eq:time-reversal-spin}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{T}$ is the time-reversal operator, not the superscript
$\mathsf{T}$ used for a matrix transpose.  Time reversal is antiunitary, so it
complex-conjugates every scalar coefficient:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}\bigl(c\lvert\psi\rangle\bigr)
    =c^*\mathcal{T}\lvert\psi\rangle.
    \label{eq:time-reversal-antilinearity}
\end{equation}
Taking $c=i$, for which $i^*=-i$, and applying the operator product to an
arbitrary state $\lvert\phi\rangle$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}i\mathcal{T}^{-1}\lvert\phi\rangle
    =-i\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{-1}\lvert\phi\rangle
    =-i\lvert\phi\rangle.
    \label{eq:time-reversal-imaginary-unit}
\end{equation}
Because this holds for every state,
$\mathcal{T}i\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-i$.  Thus the identity expresses complex
conjugation by time reversal; it is not an ordinary matrix sign change.  For
example, in the position representation $\mathbf{p}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}$,
so complex conjugation reverses momentum:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}\mathbf{p}\mathcal{T}^{-1}
    =i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}
    =-\mathbf{p}.
    \label{eq:time-reversal-momentum}
\end{equation}
For a classical magnetization trajectory, the corresponding transformed
trajectory is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(t)=-\mathbf{m}(-t).
    \label{eq:time-reversed-magnetization}
\end{equation}
A magnetic state can therefore break time-reversal symmetry even when its
nonmagnetic crystal structure does not.  A fixed applied magnetic field, a
fixed current, damping, or another nonequilibrium drive must not be silently
left unchanged when testing time reversal; these time-odd or irreversible
ingredients generally prevent the driven equation from being
time-reversal-invariant.

\subsection{Parity or Spatial-Inversion Symmetry}

Here parity means full spatial inversion,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}:\quad \mathbf{r}\to-\mathbf{r}.
    \label{eq:spatial-inversion}
\end{equation}
For inversion, $R_{\mathrm{inv}}=-I_3$ and $\det R_{\mathrm{inv}}=-1$.
Substitution into the axial-vector rule in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:polar-axial-transformation} gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{a}'=(\det R_{\mathrm{inv}})R_{\mathrm{inv}}\mathbf{a}
    =(-1)(-I_3)\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}.
    \label{eq:axial-vector-under-inversion}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the shorthand
$\mathbf{S},\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{S},\mathbf{M}$ means that inversion does
not reverse the components or direction of these axial vectors.  It does move
each vector from its original position to the inversion-related position:
\begin{equation}
    \bigl(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})\bigr)
    \to
    \bigl(-\mathbf{r},\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})\bigr).
    \label{eq:axial-vectors-moved-by-inversion}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, when the transformed textures are written as functions of the
coordinate $\mathbf{r}$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{S}(-\mathbf{r}),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{M}(-\mathbf{r}).
    \label{eq:inverted-spin-texture}
\end{equation}
This transformation law alone does not imply
$\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{S}(-\mathbf{r})$ or
$\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{M}(-\mathbf{r})$.  Those equalities hold only
when the complete magnetic texture is itself invariant under inversion.  More
generally, the structure has inversion symmetry only when the inverted sites,
chemical species, interactions, and spin pattern reproduce the original
structure.
This is distinct from particle-exchange symmetry.  In non-Hermitian coupled-mode
models the symbol $\mathcal{P}$ may instead denote exchange of two subsystems;
its meaning must therefore be stated explicitly.

\subsection{Mirror Symmetry}

A mirror with unit normal $\mathbf{n}$ reverses the perpendicular component of
a polar vector.  An axial vector behaves oppositely: its components parallel to
the mirror plane reverse, while its perpendicular component is unchanged
according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:polar-axial-transformation}~\cite{ee237-week11}.
For the $xy$ mirror,
\begin{equation}
    (v_x,v_y,v_z)\to(v_x,v_y,-v_z),
    \qquad
    (S_x,S_y,S_z)\to(-S_x,-S_y,S_z).
    \label{eq:xy-mirror-polar-axial}
\end{equation}
The transformed lattice and every axial spin must both be compared with the
original magnetic structure.  A lattice may therefore possess a mirror plane
that is broken by its magnetic order.

\subsection{Spin-Rotation Symmetry}

Spatial rotations move the lattice, whereas spin rotations act in spin space.
For the isotropic exchange Hamiltonian used in ASTRO,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ex}}
    =\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\ne j}J_{ij}\,\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j,
    \label{eq:symmetry-isotropic-exchange}
\end{equation}
a common spin rotation $R_s$ leaves every dot product unchanged:
\begin{equation}
    (R_s\mathbf{S}_i)\cdot(R_s\mathbf{S}_j)
    =\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j.
    \label{eq:global-spin-rotation}
\end{equation}
The exchange term consequently has global spin-rotation symmetry when the
couplings $J_{ij}$ do not depend on spin direction~\cite{astro-code}.  Magnetic
anisotropy, Dzyaloshinskii--Moriya interaction, dipolar coupling, an applied
field, or another spin--orbit-coupled term can reduce this continuous symmetry
to the subset of joint spatial and spin operations that preserves the complete
Hamiltonian.

\subsection{Combined Parity--Time Symmetry in a Two-Sublattice Magnet}

For the two-sublattice order parameters defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:a-type-order-parameters}, suppose that inversion exchanges
sublattices $A$ and $B$.  Since inversion does not reverse an axial spin,
whereas time reversal does, their actions are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{array}{c|cc}
                               & \mathbf{m}  & \mathbf{n}  \\
        \hline
        \mathcal{P}            & \mathbf{m}  & -\mathbf{n} \\
        \mathcal{T}            & -\mathbf{m} & -\mathbf{n} \\
        \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} & -\mathbf{m} & \mathbf{n}
    \end{array}
    \label{eq:pt-order-parameters}
\end{equation}
Thus a compensated antiferromagnetic pattern can be invariant under the
combined operation even when it is not invariant under $\mathcal{P}$ or
$\mathcal{T}$ separately.  In a different use of parity, Zhang \emph{et al.}
define $\mathcal{P}$ as interchange of two coupled ferrimagnetic layers and
obtain parity--time-symmetric N\'eel-vector dynamics at angular-momentum
compensation~\cite{zhang2024nonhermitian}.  These two meanings should not be
conflated.

\subsection{Mini-Tutorial: From $\mathcal{P}$, $\mathcal{T}$, and
    $\mathcal{PT}$ to Spintronic Effects}

The useful question is not merely whether a crystal ``has symmetry.''  For a
candidate response
\begin{equation}
    O_i=\chi_{ij}F_j,
    \label{eq:symmetry-linear-response}
\end{equation}
one must transform the observable $O$, the drive $F$, the magnetic order, and
the complete device geometry.  A symmetry of the driven structure is allowed
to keep only tensor components that reproduce Eq.~\eqref{eq:symmetry-linear-response}
after the transformation.  Symmetry therefore decides which components may be
nonzero, but not their magnitude or microscopic origin~\cite{manchon2019sot,
    zelezny2017sot,liu2020twod-sot}.

The signatures needed below follow directly from the polar/axial distinction:

    \begin{tabular}{|p{0.25\linewidth}|p{0.16\linewidth}|p{0.16\linewidth}|p{0.31\linewidth}|}
        \hline
        Quantity                                               & $\mathcal{P}$ & $\mathcal{T}$ & Character      \\
        \hline
        $\mathbf{k},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{j}$                     & odd           &
        $\mathbf{k},\mathbf{j}$ odd; $\mathbf{E}$ even         & polar                                          \\
        \hline
        $\mathbf{S},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\mathbf{M},\mathbf{B}$ & even          & odd           & axial          \\
        \hline
        spin current $Q_i^{\,a}\sim v_iS_a$                    & odd           & even          &
        polar flow index $i$, axial spin index $a$                                                              \\
        \hline
        Hall vector $\mathbf{h}$ in $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm H}=\mathbf{h}\times\mathbf{E}$
                                                               & even          & odd           & axial response \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The electric field is time-reversal even, whereas the dissipative current that
it produces is time-reversal odd.  A dc transport coefficient must therefore
be interpreted as a nonequilibrium response, not as a new equilibrium order
parameter.

\paragraph{Band-degeneracy checkpoint.}
Both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ reverse $\mathbf{k}$, so their product
leaves each crystal momentum fixed.  For spin-$1/2$ electrons with
$(\mathcal{PT})^2=-1$, $\mathcal{PT}$ produces a Kramers-like partner at the
same $\mathbf{k}$.  Consequently, a $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric collinear magnet
cannot have a same-$\mathbf{k}$ spin splitting.  Breaking $\mathcal{PT}$ is
necessary for such a splitting, but it is not sufficient: another unitary or
antiunitary symmetry may still protect degeneracy~\cite{smejkal2022altermagnet,
    jungwirth2026altermagnet-symmetry}.

\subsubsection{Rashba Spin--Orbit Coupling: Break $\mathcal{P}$, Keep
    $\mathcal{T}$}

For an interface or polar crystal with polar axis $\mathbf{n}$, the leading
isotropic momentum-linear term is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm R}
    =\alpha_{\mathrm R}(\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{k})\cdot
    \boldsymbol{\sigma}.
    \label{eq:symmetry-rashba}
\end{equation}
Time reversal changes both $\mathbf{k}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in sign, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:symmetry-rashba} is $\mathcal{T}$ even.  Inversion changes
$\mathbf{k}$ but not the axial spin; moreover an inversion-symmetric crystal
cannot possess the fixed polar axis $\mathbf{n}$.  Thus a nonzero Rashba
coefficient requires inversion asymmetry, while time reversal may remain a
symmetry.  The eigenvalues
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})
    =\varepsilon_0(\mathbf{k})
    \pm\alpha_{\mathrm R}\lvert\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{k}\rvert
    \label{eq:rashba-splitting}
\end{equation}
are split at a general $\mathbf{k}$ but obey
$\varepsilon_{+}(\mathbf{k})=\varepsilon_{-}(-\mathbf{k})$, as required by
$\mathcal{T}$~\cite{manchon2015rashba}.

\subsubsection{Dzyaloshinskii--Moriya Interaction: Break Bond-Center
    Inversion}

For one bond,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{ij}^{\mathrm{DMI}}
    =-\mathbf{D}_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf{S}_i\times\mathbf{S}_j).
    \label{eq:symmetry-dmi-bond}
\end{equation}
The directed bond and DMI-vector convention are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:atomistic-bond-interactions}.
If inversion about the bond midpoint exchanges $i$ and $j$, the axial spins do
not reverse but their ordered cross product does:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}:\quad
    \mathbf{S}_i\times\mathbf{S}_j
    \longrightarrow
    \mathbf{S}_j\times\mathbf{S}_i
    =-\mathbf{S}_i\times\mathbf{S}_j.
\end{equation}
Invariance would then require $\mathbf{D}_{ij}=-\mathbf{D}_{ij}$, hence
$\mathbf{D}_{ij}=0$.  DMI therefore needs the relevant local bond-center
inversion to be absent; the whole crystal need not be globally polar.  Under
time reversal both spins change sign, leaving their cross product and DMI
unchanged.  DMI is consequently compatible with $\mathcal{T}$ and is selected
primarily by spatial symmetry~\cite{astro-code,kong2026spatially}.

\subsubsection{Spin Hall and Anomalous Hall Effects: Similar Geometry,
    Different $\mathcal{T}$ Requirement}

In an isotropic spin Hall material the leading response can be written
\begin{equation}
    Q_i^{\,a}=\sigma_{\mathrm{SH}}\epsilon_{iaj}E_j.
    \label{eq:symmetry-spin-hall}
\end{equation}
Both $Q_i^{\,a}$ and $E_j$ are odd under inversion, while both are even under
time reversal.  Neither $\mathcal{P}$ nor $\mathcal{T}$ must therefore be
broken for the spin Hall effect; crystal mirrors and rotations only restrict
the allowed tensor components.  This is why a centrosymmetric nonmagnetic
heavy metal can generate a transverse spin current~\cite{sot-stt-compact-model,
    liu2012ta-spin-hall,pai2012w-spin-hall,davidson2020spin-currents}.

For the anomalous Hall effect,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{AH}}=\mathbf{h}\times\mathbf{E},
    \label{eq:symmetry-anomalous-hall}
\end{equation}
where the Hall vector $\mathbf{h}$ is axial and time-reversal odd.  Inversion
does not forbid it, but $\mathcal{T}$ does.  Because $\mathcal{PT}$ also changes
$\mathbf{h}$ in sign while leaving its spatial point fixed, $\mathcal{PT}$
forbids the anomalous Hall vector.  Conversely, breaking $\mathcal{T}$ and
$\mathcal{PT}$ is only a necessary screen: a mirror, rotation, or another
antiunitary symmetry can still force a particular component of $\mathbf{h}$
to vanish.  Compensated magnets can therefore have an anomalous Hall effect
without a net moment when their magnetic point group allows a time-odd axial
vector~\cite{smejkal2022ahe}.

\subsubsection{Edelstein Effect: Uniform Versus Staggered Spin Accumulation}

The Edelstein or inverse spin-galvanic response is
\begin{equation}
    S_i=\chi_{ij}E_j.
    \label{eq:symmetry-edelstein}
\end{equation}
Spin is inversion even and the electric field is inversion odd, so a global
uniform tensor $\chi_{ij}$ must vanish when inversion is a symmetry.  In a
polar film with approximate $C_{\infty v}$ symmetry, the leading form is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}=\chi_{\mathrm E}\,\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}.
    \label{eq:edelstein-isotropic}
\end{equation}
The underlying material may preserve time reversal: the spin density is a
nonequilibrium, current-odd response rather than an equilibrium magnetization.

Global inversion does not rule out a \emph{staggered} Edelstein effect.  If
inversion exchanges two locally noncentrosymmetric magnetic sites, symmetry
can impose
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}_A=-\mathbf{S}_B,
    \label{eq:staggered-edelstein}
\end{equation}
so that the unit-cell-average spin vanishes while a N\'eel-order-effective
staggered field survives.  This is the key distinction between global and
local inversion asymmetry~\cite{zelezny2017sot,watanabe2018afm-switching,
    manchon2019sot}.

\subsubsection{Spin--Orbit Torque: Convert a Symmetry-Allowed Spin Response
    into Torque}

Once a current produces a spin polarization $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$, exchange
coupling to a magnetic order permits the two familiar lowest-order torques
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{SOT}}
    =\tau_{\mathrm{FL}}\,\mathbf{m}\times\boldsymbol{\zeta}
    +\tau_{\mathrm{DL}}\,\mathbf{m}\times
    (\mathbf{m}\times\boldsymbol{\zeta}).
    \label{eq:symmetry-sot}
\end{equation}
The shared device axes and the two torque vector factors are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:sot-llgs-geometry}.
The source symmetry determines $\boldsymbol{\zeta}$: a conventional isotropic
bilayer gives $\boldsymbol{\zeta}\parallel\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{E}$,
whereas lower crystal symmetry allows additional components.  Field-like and
damping-like angular forms do not uniquely identify Rashba/Edelstein and spin
Hall microscopic mechanisms, because either mechanism can contribute to both
forms~\cite{manchon2019sot,zhang2024revisiting,xu2023field-free-afm}.

A single vertical mirror illustrates how an unconventional component appears.
Take the surviving mirror to be the $yz$ plane.  Then $E_x\to-E_x$, while the
axial component $\zeta_z\to-\zeta_z$, so $\zeta_z\propto E_x$ is allowed.  For
current inside the mirror plane, $E_y\to E_y$ but $\zeta_z\to-\zeta_z$, so the
same response is forbidden.  This is the symmetry logic behind the
current-direction-dependent out-of-plane torque in WTe$_2$/ferromagnet
bilayers~\cite{ee237-week11,macneill2017wte2,liu2020twod-sot}.

For antiferromagnets, global inversion can forbid the uniform torque while
local inversion asymmetry permits opposite sublattice torques.  Whether the
even or odd response is staggered depends on the operation that exchanges the
sublattices; local noncentrosymmetry is necessary but not sufficient, so the
full site and magnetic point groups must be checked~\cite{zelezny2017sot,
    watanabe2018afm-switching}.

\subsubsection{Spin-Transfer Torque: No Intrinsic Inversion-Breaking
    Requirement}

Spin-transfer torque transfers angular momentum from a spin-polarized current
whose polarization $\mathbf{p}$ is prepared by a magnetic polarizer, magnetic
texture, or spin filter:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{STT}}
    =u_{\mathrm{DL}}\,\mathbf{m}\times(\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{m})
    +u_{\mathrm{FL}}\,\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{p}.
    \label{eq:symmetry-stt}
\end{equation}
Unlike Rashba SOC, DMI, or a uniform Edelstein response, conventional STT does
not require spin--orbit coupling or broken crystal inversion.  Its essential
ingredients are a nonequilibrium spin flux and a noncollinear receiving
magnetization.  Magnetic order or spin filtering supplies the time-reversal-
odd polarization $\mathbf{p}$, while reversing the current reverses the
angular-momentum flux.  Device geometry can of course break inversion, but
that is not the microscopic selection rule for STT~\cite{sot-stt-compact-model,
    yuan2023thermal,zhu2019voltage}.

\subsubsection{Altermagnetic Spin Splitting: Break $\mathcal{T}$ Without
    $\mathcal{PT}$ Degeneracy}

In a conventional collinear antiferromagnet, opposite-spin sublattices are
often related by inversion or a translation.  A $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry then
protects same-$\mathbf{k}$ degeneracy directly.  In the nonrelativistic spin
group, a spin flip combined with a sublattice translation can likewise make
the two spin channels degenerate throughout the Brillouin zone; after
spin--orbit coupling is included, translation--time-reversal alone generally
protects degeneracy only at selected momenta.  An altermagnet instead relates
the opposite-spin sublattices by a proper or improper crystal rotation
$\mathcal{R}$, but not by a translation or inversion based spin-group
operation that would restore general same-$\mathbf{k}$ degeneracy.
Schematically,
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})
    =\varepsilon_{\downarrow}(\mathcal{R}\mathbf{k}),
    \qquad
    \varepsilon_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{k})
    \ne\varepsilon_{\downarrow}(\mathbf{k})
    \quad\text{at a general }\mathbf{k}.
    \label{eq:altermagnetic-splitting}
\end{equation}
The rotation redistributes the two spin channels so that the net moment can
still compensate.  The resulting even-parity $d$-, $g$-, or $i$-wave spin
splitting is nonrelativistic and does not require spin--orbit coupling;
spin--orbit coupling subsequently modifies nodal degeneracies and enables
additional Berry-curvature responses.  Inversion may still exist as an
operation within a same-spin sector, but it must not exchange the two
opposite-spin sublattices in the way that restores $\mathcal{PT}$ degeneracy
~\cite{smejkal2022altermagnet,smejkal2022altermagnet-landscape,
    jungwirth2026altermagnet-symmetry}.

\subsection{Practical Symmetry Test}

To test a proposed symmetry of a spin model:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item map every position and sublattice label;
    \item transform spin as an axial vector and reverse it additionally under
          time reversal;
    \item transform external fields, currents, and boundary conditions;
    \item rebuild every Hamiltonian or dynamical term with the transformed
          quantities; and
    \item compare the complete transformed model and magnetic pattern with the
          originals.
\end{enumerate}
Checking only the atomic lattice or only the spin arrows is insufficient for a
magnetic symmetry.
